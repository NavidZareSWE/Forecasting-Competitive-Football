      01 What this project actually is

      In one sentence: take a raw, second-by-second log of professional football matches, and build three models that forecast the result — one before kick-off, one for the goal difference, and one that keeps updating while the match is being played — then prove your forecasts are better than a naive guess and honestly compare them to the bookmakers.

      The story behind it

      Imagine you are sitting in a television studio five minutes before kick-off. On screen there is a graphic: Home 54% · Draw 25% · Away 21%. Where did those numbers come from? Somebody built a pre-match model: it looked at everything known about the two teams beforehand — recent form, playing style, squad strength, who is at home, how many rest days they had — and produced a probability for each of the three possible results.

      Now the match starts. In the 55th minute the home team's centre-back is sent off. The graphic changes instantly. That is a second, different model: an in-play model. It is watching the stream of events as they happen (pass, shot, foul, card, goal) and re-estimating the same three probabilities every few minutes.

      PDF The PDF puts it exactly this way: pre-match models produce opening bookmaker prices and pre-game analytics; in-play models are the engine behind live betting markets and the win-probability graphics on a broadcast. Both answer the same questions, but they live on completely different data pipelines. Building both, honestly and without leakage, is described as the heart of this project.

      Why it is a hard project, and where the marks really are

      The modelling itself — calling XGBoost — is the easy part, and the grading reflects that. Nothing is handed to you as a neat spreadsheet. You are given a tree of nested JSON files, roughly four thousand recorded events per match, and you must decide what a single training row even is before you can train anything.

      03 Concepts you need before you start

      Nothing below assumes prior machine-learning knowledge. Each idea is given once in plain language, then once precisely. If you already know a term, skip its box.

      3.1 Supervised learning in one paragraph

      Intuition. Imagine a huge table. Each row is one thing that happened in the past. The columns on the left describe the situation; the single column on the right records how it turned out. You show a computer thousands of these rows and ask it to find the pattern that maps left to right. Then you hand it a new row with the right-hand column erased, and it guesses.

      Precisely. The left-hand columns are the features (a vector x). The right-hand column is the label or target (y). One row is an example. Training means fitting a function f(x) → ŷ on the historic rows so that it generalises to unseen ones.

      The question that gets asked at every defence

      PDF “For each model, your report must state precisely what one input example is, what the output is, and how the training set is constructed, before any modeling begins. This problem-definition step is graded.” For this project the answer is different for each of the three models, and for Model 3 it is genuinely subtle. See section 05.

      3.2 Classification vs regression

      Classification

      The label is one of a small set of categories. Here: Home win / Draw / Away win — three classes. A good classifier does not just pick one; it outputs a probability for each, e.g. [0.54, 0.25, 0.21], summing to 1.

      Regression

      The label is a number on a continuous scale. Here: the goal margin, home score minus away score. A 3–1 home win has margin +2; a 0–2 defeat has margin −2.

      The TA's aside about the two being cousins

      He remarked that classification and regression are not really different in spirit: the margin, once you clip it to the range [−5, +5], only takes a handful of distinct integer values, so you could equally see it as a classification problem over those values. (Clipping to [−5,+5] leaves the eleven integers −5…+5; the TA said “ten states” while gesturing at the idea, not counting them.) He is making a conceptual point — the deliverable in the PDF is still a regression, evaluated with MAE and RMSE.

      3.3 Probability calibration — the idea this whole project is graded on

      Intuition. Think about a weather forecaster. Over the last year she said “70% chance of rain” on one hundred days. If it actually rained on roughly seventy of them, she is calibrated: her 70% means 70%. If it rained on only thirty, she is confidently wrong, and you should stop trusting her even if she “gets the answer right” (rain vs no rain) more often than you do.

      Precisely. A model is calibrated when, among all predictions with confidence p, the empirical frequency of the event is also p. Two tools measure this:

      • Reliability diagram. Bucket predictions by confidence on the x-axis; plot the observed frequency on the y-axis. Perfect calibration is the 45° diagonal.
      • ECE (Expected Calibration Error). The average gap between confidence and accuracy across buckets, weighted by bucket size. Lower is better.
      ECE = Σm=1..M ( |Bm| / N ) · | acc(Bm) − conf(Bm) |

      PDF The brief says the project “weights calibration and proper scoring rules heavily”, because “a model that confidently predicts a home win and is wrong must be punished more than one that expressed appropriate uncertainty.”

      TA

      “While you check the accuracy, check how good the calibration is. How well are the probabilities speaking?” He explicitly told students to look at the calibration plots visually, not just at a single accuracy number.

      3.4 The scoring rules you will report

      A proper scoring rule is a score that is minimised (best) only when you report your true beliefs — you cannot cheat it by exaggerating confidence.

      Ranked Probability Score (RPS) — the headline metric for Model 1

      Intuition. The three outcomes sit on a natural ladder: Home win → Draw → Away win. If the true result is a Home win, predicting a Draw is a smaller mistake than predicting an Away win — you were only one rung off. RPS is the scoring rule that knows about the rungs. Ordinary accuracy does not.

      Precisely. With r = 3 ordered categories, forecast probabilities pj and outcome indicators ej (one-hot), RPS compares the cumulative distributions:

      RPS = ( 1 / (r − 1) ) · Σi=1..r−1 [ Σj=1..i ( pj − ej ) ]2
      LaTeX source, for pasting into your report 
      \mathrm{RPS} \;=\; \frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i} \left(p_j - e_j\right)\right)^{2}

      Lower is better; 0 is a perfect, fully confident, correct forecast. The PDF's reference [10] (Constantinou & Fenton, 2012, Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports) is the paper that argues RPS is the right scoring rule for football forecasts — cite it when you justify the metric.

      Log-Loss and Brier Score — the two secondary metrics

      Log-Loss punishes confident mistakes brutally (it goes to infinity as you assign probability 0 to something that then happens). Brier Score is a gentler squared error on the probability vector.

      LogLoss = −(1/N) Σn Σk onk · ln pnk      Brier = (1/N) Σn Σk ( pnk − onk )2

      onk is 1 if example n really had outcome k, else 0. Unlike RPS, neither of these knows the outcomes are ordered.

      MAE and RMSE — for Model 2's margin

      Mean Absolute Error is the average size of your miss in goals. Root Mean Squared Error is the same idea but squares the errors first, so it is dominated by the occasional 5–0 you failed to see coming. Report both, plus the correlation between predicted and actual margins.

      3.5 Data leakage — the failure mode that destroys sports-prediction projects

      Intuition. Leakage is studying for tomorrow's exam using tomorrow's answer sheet. You will score brilliantly in practice and fail in the exam hall, because the answer sheet will not be there. In this project, the answer sheet is the future.

      Precisely. PDF “Leakage occurs whenever information that would not be available at prediction time influences the model. For the pre-match models the prediction time is kick-off; for the in-play model it is the snapshot time t.”

      It gets its own section — section 06 — because event data multiplies the opportunities to leak, and because it is the thing the TA will interrogate you about at Mid Defence 1.

      3.7 SHAP, in one analogy

      Four friends share a taxi. They split the fare not equally but by how much distance each person actually added to the trip. That fair-split rule from cooperative game theory is a Shapley value. SHAP applies it to a prediction: each feature receives a signed number saying how much it pushed this specific prediction up or down from the model's average output, and those numbers add up exactly to the gap.

      PDF “A forecast that cannot be explained cannot be trusted” — SHAP is a required deliverable and the centrepiece of the final defence. See section 11.

      04 The data: two sources, one join

      Everything you model comes from two places. One gives you what happened on the pitch. The other gives you what the world believed would happen before kick-off. Neither is a table. Making them into one is worth 30% of the project.

      4.2 The integration challenge — the first graded deliverable

      PDF “This dataset is deliberately not a flat table. Turning it into modeling-ready data is the first graded deliverable of the project, and it is substantial.” You must:

      • Parse the JSON tree and join the five file families on their shared keys.
      • Resolve player and team identities across seasons and competitions (the same club, the same player, appearing in different files across 20 years).
      • Materialise clean tables from which both feature pipelines can be built.
      • Define the problem formally: what is one example, what is its label, which rows of which files may contribute to it.
      • Document the imperfections, do not hide them: 360 files exist only for some matches; some competitions are single tournaments while others are full league seasons; optional event fields appear only for certain event types.

      Competition selection is a decision you must defend

      Verbatim requirement

      “You must justify which competitions you train on. Full league seasons (La Liga's 18 seasons are the backbone) give volume and temporal depth for the pre-match models; tournaments add variety but little history. Mixing them naively creates distribution shift that you must measure, not ignore.”

      Distribution shift means the statistical character of the data changes between groups — a 1973/74 La Liga match, a 2024 Copa America match and a 2015/16 Premier League match are not draws from the same population. If you train on one and test on another without saying so, your numbers describe nothing.

      Where features come from

      The PDF's own list of ideas, and the crucial observation about how each one becomes two different features:

      Aggregated over a team's prior matches → pre-match features

      Shot counts and shot locations (an xG proxy), pressure intensity, passing volume and completion by zone, carries into the final third, set-piece counts, possession share, defensive actions. Plus: rolling form, venue (home/away), rest days, head-to-head.

      Computed over a rolling window inside the current match → in-play features

      The same event statistics, but only from this match, only up to minute t: current score, man-advantage (red cards), event counts and rates in a recent window, momentum indicators.

      xG (expected goals) is the probability that a given shot becomes a goal, given where and how it was taken. You are not required to build a real xG model; the PDF calls shot counts and locations an “xG proxy”.

      football-data.co.uk  (free per-season CSVs; England: /englandm.php, Spain: /spainm.php, and equivalent pages for Germany, Italy, France and others)

      What you must do with the odds — four graded steps

      1. Download. Pick the league pages matching your chosen StatsBomb competitions and download every season CSV that overlaps your data.
      2. Tag to match_id. There is no shared key. You must join on (date, home team, away team) and resolve team-name aliases between the two sources (“Man United” vs “Manchester United”). Document the mapping and its failure cases.

      5.1 A required extra analysis on Model 2

      Easy to miss

      “You must also analyse whether Model 2's margin can be converted into useful Model 1 probabilities.” That is: given a predicted margin of, say, +0.4 goals, can you turn that number into a sensible (Home, Draw, Away) distribution, and how does it compare to Model 1 trained directly?       5.2 Model 3, the hardest one, explained slowly

      Imagine that you freeze the broadcast at minute 30. Everything visible on the screen at that instant — the score, whether anyone has been sent off, who has been dominating the last ten minutes — plus everything you knew before kick-off, becomes one row of your training table. Its label is the result the match will eventually have. Now unfreeze, run to minute 35, freeze again: another row, same label. One 90-minute match, snapshotted every five minutes, produces roughly eighteen rows.

      That is a snapshot, and designing the snapshotting scheme is your decision to make and defend.

      PDF Model 3 is “deliberately the hardest deliverable”. It forces you to:

      • define a snapshotting scheme;
      • prevent within-match leakage (no event after time t may influence the snapshot at t);
      • keep all snapshots of one match on one side of the train/test boundary;
      • reason about calibration per game phase.

      How Model 3 is evaluated — the shape of the curve is the result

      PDF “Evaluation must show performance as a function of match minute: as information arrives, Model 3 must increasingly outperform its own pre-match prior, and its probabilities must converge toward certainty as full time approaches.”

      Concretely: draw a graph with match minute on the x-axis and, say, RPS on the y-axis. Draw two curves — your in-play model, and the frozen pre-match prediction (the model 1 output, held constant across the whole match, as a flat reference line). At minute 0 they should coincide. By minute 85 the in-play curve should be far below it. If it is not, you have a finding to explain, not a failure to hide.

      The TA's intuition for why Model 3 matters

      “Before the game, from the players, the coach, the team's history, its rank in the table, you have a prediction: seventy percent this one wins, ten percent that one, twenty percent a draw. But during the game someone might suddenly get a red card — and then it makes a huge difference.” The whole point of the live model is to react to that card.

      He also framed Task L as Task C repeated: “task three is task one that keeps changing.” Same three probabilities, re-asked every few minutes.

      06 Data leakage: the eight absolute rules

      This is the section that decides whether your results mean anything. PDF Hard rule “The rules below are absolute.” Every item must answer YES before submission.

      6.1 Why each rule exists

      1. Pre-kickoff features only (Models 1 and 2)

      Form and event-rate aggregates must summarise the past. A rolling average that accidentally includes the target match leaks the very outcome you are predicting, and produces “a model that is unbeatable on paper and useless in reality.”

      TA, saying the same thing his way

      “The feature must be present, and the feature must be calculable at that moment when its timestamp is. For example, the number of goals scored up to after the next match shouldn't suddenly be there — it doesn't exist at that time. Do you get what I'm saying?” He also reminds you that although the joined data becomes one tabular CSV, “this CSV also has a time” — your test set must never sit in the past while your training set draws on the future.

      2. The time-t boundary (Model 3)

      “This is the sharpest trap in the project. Event files contain the whole match; when you snapshot at minute 30, a single careless join can pull in the 70th-minute goal.” The prescribed defence, verbatim: sort events by period, timestamp and index; cut strictly at t; and unit-test the cut with an assertion that no contributing event postdates the snapshot.

      What that assertion looks like in practice 
      def snapshot(events_df, match_id, t_seconds):
    ev = events_df[events_df.match_id == match_id]
    ev = ev.sort_values(["period", "timestamp", "index"])
    cut = ev[ev.elapsed_seconds <= t_seconds]

    # the leakage assertion the PDF asks for
    assert cut.elapsed_seconds.max() <= t_seconds, "snapshot contains a future event"
    assert len(cut) == 0 or cut.index.is_monotonic_increasing

    return build_features(cut)     # <- the ONLY thing feature code may see

      The point is not the exact code. The point is that the cut happens once, in one function, and that function is the only door through which events reach your features. If two code paths can build features, one of them will eventually leak.

      3. Match-level splitting

      Snapshots from the same match share a label and are heavily correlated. “If minute-30 of a match sits in training and minute-60 of the same match sits in test, the test score is fiction.” Split by match (and chronologically by season), then let snapshots inherit their parent match's side.

      4. Resample inside folds

      Fitting SMOTE on the full dataset lets synthetic minority points be built from test-set neighbours, so information crosses the split. On temporal data it is worse: synthetic samples can be interpolated from future matches. The PDF's instruction is specific: use the imbalanced-learn Pipeline — not scikit-learn's — so resampling is confined to training folds automatically.

      5. Fit transforms on training data only

      A scaler or imputer fit on the whole dataset has already seen the test distribution. Fit on train; transform validation and test with those frozen parameters.

      6. No post-snapshot information in a snapshot

      Obvious once stated, easy to violate: total shots in the match, final possession share, and the result itself are all forbidden inside a minute-30 row.

      7. Odds: baseline or feature, never both

      Read this one twice — sources differ in emphasis

      07 The market baseline, and how to de-vig it

      The bookmakers' odds are not a model you build. They are the number your model is measured against. To use them you must first strip out the bookmaker's profit margin.

      7.1 Decimal odds, and why they are not probabilities

      Intuition. Decimal odds of 2.00 mean: stake 1, get 2 back if you win. That looks like a 50% chance. Odds of 4.00 look like 25%. Now take the TA's real example from the Arsenal match: home 1.53, draw 4.50, away 6.50. Convert each with 1/odds and add them up.

      1/1.53 + 1/4.50 + 1/6.50 = 0.6536 + 0.2222 + 0.1538 = 1.0297

      The three “probabilities” sum to more than one. That surplus of about 3% is the bookmaker's built-in margin, called the over-round or the vig (vigorish). It is how the shop makes money regardless of the result. Raw 1/odds values are therefore implied probabilities, not fair ones.

      7.2 De-vigging: normalise the three to sum to one

      PDF “Convert each of the three 1X2 odds to its raw implied probability (one divided by the decimal odd) and normalise the three so they sum to exactly one.”

      p̂k = ( 1 / ok )  ÷  Σj ∈ {H,D,A} ( 1 / oj )
      LaTeX source + the worked example, ready for your report 
      \hat{p}_k \;=\; \frac{1/o_k}{\sum_{j\in\{H,D,A\}} 1/o_j}, \qquad k \in \{H, D, A\}

      Worked on the TA's numbers (bet365: 1.53 / 4.50 / 6.50):

      raw   = [1/1.53, 1/4.50, 1/6.50] = [0.6536, 0.2222, 0.1538]
sum   = 1.0297                      # over-round = 2.97%
p_hat = raw / 1.0297                = [0.6348, 0.2158, 0.1494]
                                      #  63.5% H   21.6% D   14.9% A  -> sums to 1.000

      You may de-vig a single chosen bookmaker or an average across bookmakers — the PDF permits either, and requires you to say which you did.

      The TA described exactly this procedure, with an averaging step

      “If there were five sites and we have five odds for home win, you take one divided by the average of those five — it becomes a number, say 0.30, you say thirty percent. Then you calculate for the next one, it becomes fifty percent, the other becomes forty percent. It becomes more than a hundred — say a hundred and twenty percent. Then you divide everything by a hundred and twenty.”

      Same algorithm, described with round numbers: implied probabilities, then normalise by their sum. His averaging happens on the odds across bookmakers before inverting.

      Why kernel methods blow up — the handshake analogy

      If every person at a party must shake hands with every other person, n people produce about n²/2 handshakes. A kernel method must compute a similarity between every pair of training examples, so its kernel matrix has n² entries. With 380 matches that is trivial. With a snapshot table of, say, 5,000 matches × 18 snapshots = 90,000 rows, the exact kernel matrix has 8.1 billion entries. That is the O(n²) space cost the PDF asks you to derive and then show on a plot by timing training runs at increasing dataset sizes.

      This is also why the PDF pairs Kernel Ridge with RBFSampler / Nystroem: those approximate the kernel and let you compare exact vs. approximate.

      10 Imbalance and resampling

      Football outcomes are not evenly split. Across large samples the PDF gives: home wins ~45%, away wins ~28%, draws ~27% — and the mix drifts by competition and era.

      That is a mild imbalance compared with, say, fraud detection, but it is real — and for the in-play task it changes shape over the match: “snapshots taken while a team leads by two goals are dominated by one eventual outcome.” How you handle imbalance interacts with calibration in ways the PDF insists “must be studied rather than assumed.”

      10.1 The six arms you must run

      Run all of these on identical splits, and compare them on both aggregate and per-class metrics, for the pre-match classification task at minimum:

      ArmWhat it does
      VanillaTrain on the raw class distribution with no resampling — the honest baseline.
      SMOTESynthetic Minority Over-sampling: invents new minority-class rows by interpolating between a minority point and its nearest minority neighbours. Fit inside training folds only.
      BorderlineSMOTEConcentrates the synthetic samples near the decision boundary, where errors cluster.
      ADASYNAdaptively synthesises more samples for the harder minority regions.
      Class weightsPass class_weight='balanced' to the estimators instead of resampling, and compare the trade-off.
      Your P1 methodIf P1 addresses imbalance or data generation, include it as an additional arm of this study.

      10.2 How to evaluate them

      Compare every method using both aggregate scores (RPS, Log-Loss) and per-class precision, recall and F1, and plot a reliability diagram for each.

      The lesson the PDF wants you to find

      “A recurring lesson is that improving minority-class recall through aggressive oversampling often degrades probability calibration, so this trade-off must be measured and discussed rather than ignored.” In this project, calibration is what you are graded on. A resampling scheme that lifts draw-recall while wrecking your ECE is a result, and you should report it as one.

      Three ways resampling becomes leakage
      1. Fitting SMOTE on the full dataset before splitting is data leakage and will be penalised.
      2. Use the imbalanced-learn Pipeline — not scikit-learn's — so resampling never touches validation or test data.
      3. Never oversample the snapshot table across matches: synthetic snapshots interpolated between two different matches are physically meaningless and leak label information.
      Illustrative sketch — resampling that cannot leak 
      from imblearn.pipeline import Pipeline        # NOT sklearn.pipeline
from imblearn.over_sampling import SMOTE
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold

pipe = Pipeline([
    ("scale",  StandardScaler()),   # fit on the training fold only
    ("smote",  SMOTE()),            # applied inside the fold only
    ("model",  clf),
])

# groups = match_id  ->  all snapshots of a match stay together
cv = GroupKFold(n_splits=5)
for tr, va in cv.split(X, y, groups=match_ids):
    pipe.fit(X[tr], y[tr])

      Two protections in one snippet: the imblearn pipeline confines SMOTE and the scaler to the training fold, and groups=match_ids keeps every snapshot of a match on one side. For the real project, the split must additionally be chronological, not random — see the temporal-splitting stage in section 12. Verify signatures against the imbalanced-learn and scikit-learn docs.

      “The API must reuse the training feature pipeline, not a reimplementation. A fast endpoint that computes features differently from training is a deployment bug, and the TA will check for it by comparing an API response against your offline prediction for the same snapshot.”

      Suggested stack: FastAPI with uvicorn, a preloaded model object, an in-memory state store per streamed match. No database required.

      Illustrative sketch — the shape of the endpoint 
      from fastapi import FastAPI
from pydantic import BaseModel

app = FastAPI()

MODEL   = load_model("model3.pkl")      # loaded ONCE, at import time
PRECOMP = {}                            # match_id -> cached pre-match vector

class Snapshot(BaseModel):
    match_id: int
    t_seconds: int

@app.post("/predict")
def predict(req: Snapshot):
    pre  = PRECOMP[req.match_id]                     # cached, not recomputed
    live = incremental_state(req.match_id, req.t_seconds)   # same code as training
    x    = assemble(pre, live)                       # the SAME assemble() as training
    p    = MODEL.predict_proba(x)[0]
    m    = MODEL_MARGIN.predict(x)[0]
    return {"home": p[0], "draw": p[1], "away": p[2], "expected_margin": m}

      The single most important line is assemble() being imported from your training package rather than rewritten. Verify FastAPI usage against the official documentation.

      19 Frequently asked questions

      Do I really have to train every model in the table, on every task?

      Yes. The Task column tells you where each applies (kernel SVM: C and R; Kernel Ridge: R only; the tree models and P2: C, R and L). “No cherry-picking: report the complete metric suite for every model on every task, including where performance is poor.”

      Can I use a neural network anywhere?

      P2 explicitly excludes them: “no neural networks, transformers, or deep sequence models.” The TA extended the same exclusion in conversation: “everything except deep learning and transformers — anything that can be done with ML, you can do.” Treat deep learning as out of scope for this project.

      What if my model can't beat the bookmakers?

      That is the expected outcome, and it is fine. “Beating the market consistently is a very high bar, and honest, well-analysed failure to beat it is worth more marks than an unexplained score that merely looks good.” What is not fine is failing to place your number next to the market's number on the same matches.

      My model does beat the bookmakers. Should I be happy?

      Be suspicious first. Walk the eight leakage rules one by one. Check the odds are not in your features. Check the rolling averages exclude the target match. Check no snapshot of a test match sits in training. Check the scaler was fit on train only. Only then be happy.

      Which competitions should I train on?

      Your choice — but you must justify it, and the justification is examined. The PDF points at La Liga's 18 seasons as “the backbone” for volume and temporal depth. Tournaments add variety but little history, and mixing naively creates distribution shift “that you must measure, not ignore.” Remember that your test set is further constrained: it may only contain matches you successfully tagged with odds, and Football-Data.co.uk covers the major European leagues, not international tournaments.

      What about matches with no 360 data?

      360 exists for a subset only. Three legitimate routes, all mentioned in the sources: drop the 360-derived features entirely; impute them (an explicitly acceptable P1 direction: “imputation strategies for matches lacking 360 data”); or augment. TA He raised exactly this: “if you want to use the 360 data in training, many of your matches don't have that feature — can you augment? What do you want to do?” Whatever you choose, it is a defence question at Mid Defence 1.

      How often should I snapshot?

      The PDF's example is “one snapshot every five minutes”, and calls the scheme yours to design. Be ready for the Defence 2 question: “predict what would change if snapshots were taken every minute instead of every five.” Think about row count, within-match correlation, compute cost, and whether the extra rows carry new information.

      What happens if a snapshot straddles half-time?

      The PDF names this as a what-if the TA will ask at Defence 1. It does not prescribe an answer — it expects you to have one, and to have implemented it consistently. (Events carry a period field; your ordering key is (period, timestamp, index).)

      Can I use AI to write my code, my dashboard, or my report?

      TA “Even if you move forward with AI, do it — but keep in mind that you must, again I say, be able to defend it.” He said the same about the bonus dashboard. The examination is on understanding, and in two-person groups he chooses who answers.

      Is the bonus worth doing?

      It is optional bonus credit on top of the grade, but “bonus credit is awarded only if the demonstration works in the room.” Attempt it only when Model 3 and its feature code are stable, since the API must import that exact code — and the TA checks by comparing an API response with your offline prediction for the same snapshot.

      How many people per group, and does that change the work?

      Groups of 1–2. The work is identical. In a pair, both members must have commits in the git history, and either member may be asked any question at any defence.

      What is the single most likely way to fail this project?

      Leaking. Second most likely: leaving Model 3 until the last week. Third: producing beautiful numbers you cannot defend.

      20 Glossary

      Every term used in the sources, defined once.

      TermMeaning in this project
      1X2The three-way match-result market: 1 = home win, X = draw, 2 = away win.
      360 dataStatsBomb freeze-frames giving the positions of all visible players around an event. Available for selected matches only.
      AblationRemoving one component (a feature group, P1, a resampling arm) and re-measuring, to attribute the effect to that component.
      ADASYNAdaptive Synthetic sampling: generates more synthetic minority examples in the regions the classifier finds hard.
      Asian HandicapA betting market with a goal head-start; present as columns in the Football-Data.co.uk CSVs.
      BaggingTraining many models on random subsets and averaging them (Random Forest). Reduces variance.
      BoostingTraining models sequentially, each correcting its predecessors' errors (GBM, XGBoost, LightGBM).
      BorderlineSMOTESMOTE restricted to minority points near the decision boundary.
      Brier ScoreMean squared error between the predicted probability vector and the one-hot outcome. Lower is better.
      CalibrationThe property that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. Measured by ECE and reliability diagrams.
      CORE rankingA ranking of computer-science conferences (A*, A, B, C). P2 conferences must be A* or A.
      De-vigRemoving the bookmaker's margin from odds by normalising the implied probabilities to sum to one.
      Distribution shiftThe data's statistical character differing between groups (eras, competitions), so a model trained on one does not transfer.
      Dummy modelA predictor that ignores features and outputs the base rate or majority class. The sanity floor.
      ECEExpected Calibration Error: the size-weighted average gap between confidence and accuracy across confidence buckets.
      Event dataA timestamped log of every on-pitch action, with coordinates, player, team and type. ~4,000 rows per match.
      Feature / label / exampleThe inputs, the answer, and one row of the training table.
      Frozen pre-match predictionThe Model-1 output held constant through a match, used as the reference curve Model 3 must beat.
      ImputationFilling in missing values (e.g. 360-derived features for matches lacking 360 files). The imputer is fit on train only.
      In-play / livePrediction made during the match, at snapshot time t. Task L.
      Isotonic regressionA non-parametric calibration method that fits a monotone mapping from raw scores to calibrated probabilities.
      Kernel methodA model that works through pairwise similarities between examples (SVM, Kernel Ridge). Cost grows as O(n²).
      LeakageAny information reaching the model that would not exist at prediction time.
      Log-LossNegative log-likelihood of the true class. Punishes confident mistakes severely.
      MAE / RMSEMean absolute error / root mean squared error. Model 2's metrics.
      MarginSigned goal difference, home minus away, clipped to [−5, +5]. Model 2's label.
      Over-round (vig)The amount by which a bookmaker's implied probabilities exceed 1. Their built-in margin.
      Platt scalingCalibration by fitting a logistic function to the model's scores.
      Proper scoring ruleA score minimised only by reporting your true beliefs. RPS, Log-Loss and Brier are proper; accuracy is not.
      Q1 venueA journal in the top quartile of its subject category, per Scimago or JCR.
      Reliability diagramPredicted confidence (x) against observed frequency (y). The diagonal is perfect calibration.
      RPSRanked Probability Score: the proper scoring rule that respects the ordering Home → Draw → Away. Model 1's headline metric.
      Shapley value / SHAPA signed per-feature contribution to one prediction; contributions sum to the gap from the model's average output.
      SMOTESynthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique: creates new minority rows by interpolating between neighbours.
      SnapshotOne (match, time t) row for Model 3, built only from events with timestamp ≤ t.
      Temporal splitA train/validation/test division ordered in time, so training never sees the future.
      TreeSHAPThe exact, fast SHAP algorithm for tree ensembles (shap.TreeExplainer).
      xG (expected goals)The probability a shot becomes a goal. The PDF treats shot counts and locations as an xG proxy.
