Three deliverables, one question

The same match is predicted twice — once from history alone, and once from history plus everything that has happened up to minute t. Every label is derived once, from the match file, and never from odds or events.

Barrier 1 of 4

The split is a date, not a shuffle

Whole dates are assigned to a side. No random split, no stratification — a shuffled split would let a model learn February from March and then be scored on January.

TRAIN · 8962015-08-07 → 2016-01-31
VALIDATION · 2772016-02-01 → 2016-03-18
TEST · 3442016-03-19 → 2016-05-17

Asserted, not assumed

  • Every train date is strictly before every validation date, which is strictly before every test date
  • One split per match_id — and all 19 snapshots of a match inherit that match's side
  • The validation split is spent on calibration, so no hyperparameter is ever tuned on it

Why it costs us

The test window is the run-in — March to May, when title races and relegation fights distort team behaviour. That is the honest, hard version of the problem, and it is also where the bookmaker is at its sharpest. We did not pick an easy quarter.

Snapshot rows follow the match: 17,024 train · 5,263 val · 6,536 test.

Barrier 2 of 4

Two tables, two definitions of "known"

5A · pre-match

Only matches that finished first

.shift(1) → .rolling(5) # in that order
  • Rolling form over the previous 5 matches: gf · ga · xgf · xga · points · win, per side, plus the difference of each
  • rest_days and played_prior
  • Every team's first match of the season is NaN — asserted, because a non-NaN there would mean the window reached into the present
  • Shift before roll. Roll-then-shift silently includes the match being predicted
5B · in-play

Only events at or before t

events sorted by (period, index) — not timestamp
  • State at t: goal_diff · home/away goals · man_advantage · shot_diff · sot_diff · xg_diff · home/away xg · recent_xg_diff · events_so_far
  • Sorted by (period, index) deliberately — roughly 26 events across 24 matches carry corrupted 00:00:00 timestamps that would otherwise sort to the front of their period
  • 19 snapshots per match, at t ∈ {0, 5, …, 90}

Barrier 3 of 4 · the sharpest trap

The time-t cut, and why it can only err safely

  index      →   0     1     2     3     4     5     6     7     8   ...
  minute     →   0     3     7     9    10   ⚠ 0    12    14    18        ← raw: corrupted row at index 5
  effective  →   0     3     7     9    10   10    12    14    18        ← np.maximum.accumulate(minute)
                                              ▲
                                     repaired: pinned to 10, not dragged to 0

                              cut at t = 10 ─────┐
                  [ ──────────  prefix, feeds the row  ─┤ ]   excluded ───────────▶
accumulate only ever raises  ⇒  eff[i] ≥ minute[i] for every i
so  eff[i] ≤ t  ⇒  minute[i] ≤ t
The cut can only ever exclude too much. It can never include an event that happened after t.

That is the sentence that turns a claim into a proof. prefix_length_at() uses searchsorted(…, side="right"), and assert_prefix() checks both directions — nothing after t is in, nothing at-or-before t is out.

Barrier 3 · the case that surprised us

What happens at half-time

StatsBomb's minute is not globally monotonic. Period 1 runs into stoppage — 46′, 47′ — and then period 2 restarts the clock at 45′.

  • The running maximum therefore pins the opening second-half events behind the first half's last minute
  • They are absent at t = 45, and reappear at t = 50
  • This is deliberate. Under the reading "t = 45 means half-time", it is the correct cut
  • The alternative — capping period 1 at 45′ — would pull first-half stoppage events into the t = 45 snapshot. That is the direction that actually leaks
Of the two ways to be wrong at the period boundary, we chose the one that drops information over the one that invents it.
A conservative cut costs accuracy. A leaky cut costs the whole result.

P2 · the sign-off gate

Hierarchical Shrinkage

Agarwal, Tan, Ronen, Singh, Yu — “Hierarchical Shrinkage: Improving the accuracy and interpretability of tree-based models.” ICML 2022, PMLR 162:111–135.

Hard filterEvidence
Published 2022 or laterICML 2022, PMLR v162 — the publication date, not a preprint date
Q1 journal / CORE A* or AICML is CORE A* (verified on the CORE portal)
Applicable to this data and these tasksRegularises CART and forests — mixed-type tabular, small effective n
Not deep learningA closed-form transform of leaf values. No neural component anywhere
Reimplementable from the paperOne closed-form recursion, no hidden constants — confidence 9.5 / 10
Rejected — and why
  • DRF (JMLR 2022) and GPBoost (JMLR 2022 / TPAMI 2023) — correct year, correct venue, but large C++/Rcpp codebases whose approximations are not specified in the paper text. They fail the from-scratch filter, not the quality filter
  • NGBoost (ICML 2020), PGBM (KDD 2021) — fail the date filter outright
  • fabOF (BJMSP 2025) — elegant ordinal fit for H/D/A, but scored 8.0, below our 8.5 bar: the quantile conventions live only in the R source
Declared fallback

FIGS — Fast Interpretable Greedy-Tree Sums, PNAS 2025, Q1, numbered Algorithm 1, confidence 9.0. Ready if the sign-off goes against HS.

The precise claim

Implemented from the paper, against the raw tree_ arrays. sklearn grows the underlying CART and nothing else — exactly as the paper does. imodels was used to cross-check the scheme, never called to produce a result.

P2 · the core equation

Damp each split by the node it was estimated from

A leaf mean is exactly the root mean plus the increments picked up along the path. That is an identity — it telescopes, it assumes nothing:

μ(t_L) = μ(t₀) + Σ_l [ μ(t_l) − μ(t_{l−1}) ] (A.1)

The deeper a split sits, the fewer samples supported the decision to make it. HS damps each increment:

f_HS(x) = μ(t₀) + Σ_l [ μ(t_l) − μ(t_{l−1}) ] / ( 1 + λ / N(t_{l−1}) )

The divisor is the parent's count, not the child's. That annotation is the whole method: a split taken off a large node is trusted almost fully; the identical split taken off a nearly-empty node is almost ignored.

t₀ ⊃ … ⊃ t_L = root-to-leaf path · μ(t) = node mean · N(t) = node count · one parameter λ ≥ 0

   t₀   N = 800    μ₀
    │
    │   ×  1/(1 + λ/800)   ← trusted
    ▼
   t₁   N = 140    μ₁
    │
    │   ×  1/(1 + λ/140)
    ▼
   t₂   N =  12    μ₂
    │
    │   ×  1/(1 + λ/12)    ← nearly ignored
    ▼
   t₃   (leaf)     μ₃

For classification, μ(t) is the class distribution in the node rather than a scalar. Every line of the derivation holds coordinate-wise — which is why one method serves Tasks C, R and L with no per-task adaptation.

Appendix A.3 · first derivation

HS is a convex combination of the ancestor means

Set the damping factors and re-index the second sum by l ↦ l+1:

w_l = N(t_{l−1}) / ( N(t_{l−1}) + λ ), w_{L+1} := 0
f_HS(x) = Σ_{l=0..L} c_l · μ(t_l) c₀ = 1 − w₁ c_l = w_l − w_{l+1} c_L = w_L

The c_l are a probability vector.

  • They sum to 1 — the sum telescopes: (1−w₁) + (w₁−w₂) + … + w_L = 1
  • They are non-negative — u ↦ u/(u+λ) increases in u, and the path is nested, so N(t_{l−1}) ≥ N(t_l) ⇒ w_l ≥ w_{l+1}
Consequence 1 — the one worth saying aloud

Each μ(t_l) already lies on the simplex, and a convex combination of simplex points is a simplex point. So the classifier's output is a valid probability vector for any λ. No clipping. No renormalisation. The safety is structural, not patched in.

Consequence 2 — the two limits
  • λ = 0 → all w_l = 1, so c_L = 1 and every other c is 0 — plain CART
  • λ → ∞ → all w_l → 0, so c₀ → 1 — the root mean, which for Task C is exactly the Dummy prior

HS is therefore a continuous interpolation between the sanity floor and the full unshrunk tree, with λ choosing the point on that line.

Appendix A.4 · second derivation

It is not an ad-hoc formula — it is a ridge minimiser

Write the tree in its increment basis: for every non-root node t, let ψ_t(x) = 1{x ∈ t}. Only a point's ancestors are active, so the sum is the telescoping identity, and the coefficients are the increments — which is exactly what we want to penalise.

f(x) = β₀ + Σ_{t≠t₀} β_t ψ_t(x), β_t = μ(t) − μ(parent(t))

Estimate one increment δ by ridge, restricted to the parent's samples, holding μ(p) fixed. Only the N(t) points inside t contribute:

J(δ) = Σ_{i ∈ p} ( y_i − μ(p) − δ·1{x_i ∈ t} )² + λ_t δ² ∂J/∂δ = 0 ⇒ δ̂ = ( μ(t) − μ(p) ) · N(t) / ( N(t) + λ_t )

Now pick the penalty weight that reproduces the paper's rule:

λ_t = λ · N(t) / N(parent(t))
Reading the objective back out
min_β Σ_i ( y_i − β₀ − Σ_t β_t ψ_t(x_i) )² + λ · Σ_t [ N(t) / N(parent(t)) ] · β_t²

The penalty weight N(t)/N(parent(t)) is the fraction of its parent's samples that the split sent into t. So:

  • A split that cleanly halves a large node is barely penalised
  • A split that peels a handful of points off an already-small node is penalised hard

That is the entire method. Grow the tree greedily as usual, then ridge-shrink its increments in proportion to how little data supported each one. HS is the greedy, top-down solution of that objective.

P2 · what λ actually chose

The method diagnosed our own data

λ is selected from {0, 0.1, 1, 10, 25, 50, 100} by 3-fold CV inside the training rows only — the validation split is reserved for calibration, so tuning λ there would turn the calibration set into a second training set. We did not pick these values. The CV did.

λ went to opposite ends of the grid on the two tables — precisely the behaviour the paper predicts, recovered without being told.

Honest limitation, volunteered: HS cannot repair a badly grown tree. It redistributes weight along paths that CART already chose. If the split criterion picked the wrong variable, HS averages over a wrong path. It is a variance intervention, not a bias one.

Why a matrix and not a loop

TASK_SUPPORT in model_zoo.py declares this table in code, so the sweep cannot silently run a model on a task where the brief did not specify it. Model builders all take (random_state, **params), so the tuner and the sweep share one definition of every model.

The one deliberate omission

Kernel methods are pre-match only, by design. An exact Gram matrix on the 17,024 in-play training rows is 2,211 MB. We do not pay that cost inside the sweep — we measure it as a separate deliverable and report the curve.

A trap we chose to flag ourselves

model_zoo.py guards the xgboost and lightgbm imports in try/except. Without libomp they vanish with no warning and the zoo silently returns 4 of 6. An early result table was short by one booster for exactly this reason. It is now pinned and documented.

Modeling protocol · II

Equal budget, or the comparison means nothing

12 configurations × 3 folds = 36 fits identical for every tuned model, per task
  • Random search, not grid. A full grid would silently hand LightGBM 729 configurations and Kernel SVM 20. Random search fixes the number of fits rather than the shape of the space — the only thing "comparable budget" can honestly mean
  • CV runs inside the training split only. Validation is the calibration set; tuning there would fit hyperparameters and the calibrator on the same rows and make every reported ECE optimistic
  • GroupKFold on match_id for Task L — so minute 40 and minute 45 of the same match never straddle a fold boundary
  • dummy is excluded — deliberately given no budget rather than a free pass. It has nothing to tune
  • Tables over 8,000 rows are tuned on a seeded subsample of whole matches, declared per row in the audit CSV
288
candidate evaluations written to tuning_results.csv
24 × 12
(task, model) pairs × candidates — the equal-budget assert

The assert is the point: tuning.py fails the run if any model on a task saw a different candidate count than its peers. An unfair budget cannot survive to the results table.

Two honest declarations
  • Exact Kernel Ridge is capped at 8,000 training rows. The results table carries a subsampled flag and the actual n_train used, so its compute row cannot be misread as a full-data fit
  • P2's λ is not tuned by the outer search. It is chosen by the paper's own inner 3-fold CV. Giving HS an outer λ grid as well would hand it a larger budget than every other model

Modeling protocol · III

Calibration, and the bug it exposed

isotonic → Platt → raw fit on VALIDATION, applied to TEST
  • ECE reported before and after, per model — so the calibrator's effect is visible rather than absorbed
  • Reliability diagrams drawn twice per model, raw and calibrated
  • Task L additionally reports ECE per game phase: 0–15′, 15–30′, … 75–90′
  • Metrics are hand-rolled — RPS, Brier, log-loss, ECE, per-class P/R/F1 — because RPS on an ordered outcome is not what a generic classification report gives you
The artefact that nearly cost us the whole comparison

Isotonic regression fitted on a ~340-row validation split emits exact zeros. RPS and Brier barely notice. Log-loss exploded to 1.9–2.8 nats, which made every model look far worse than the market — for a reason that was entirely an artefact of the calibrator, not of the model.

PROBABILITY_FLOOR = 0.005 # then renormalise

Post-fix, log-loss lands at 1.05–1.22. We report this deliberately: a number that moves by 1.5 nats because of a floor is a number you have to understand before you present it.

The whole suite spans 1.387 → 1.433 MAE, and the prior sits at 1.490. Eight very different learners, given identical budgets, land within 0.05 goals of each other and beat "always predict the mean" by about 7%.

That is not eight models failing. That is the signal ceiling of pre-match features being measured eight independent ways. The goal margin before kick-off is close to irreducibly noisy, and the flatness of this table is the evidence.

Results · Task L · why the sign flips

The model hedges exactly when it should commit

The mechanism

Late in a match the model is looking at a goal difference that has all but settled the result — but its training distribution is dominated by earlier, more uncertain snapshots, where hedging was correct. So it carries that hedge into the 75–90′ window and ends up less confident than it should be, having been more confident than it should be for the previous 75 minutes.

Why this is a deliverable, not a defect report

This is exactly why the brief demands calibration per game phase rather than one number over all snapshots. A single ECE would have shown a small value and hidden both halves of the failure. The per-phase table is what makes it visible.

The honest caveat

Peak memory is a measured RSS delta, so it is noisier than the timing — exact SVR reports 55 MB at full size because sklearn's SMO never materialises the whole Gram at once. The theoretical Gram is the fair number for the design decision, and it is 2,211 MB.

What the numbers actually say

Four findings, in order of how much we trust them

  • The market wins pre-match, by 0.02 RPS, and none of our seven models closes it. Measured on identical rows with 99.71% coverage. The most defensible result in the project — because it is the one we most wanted to be different.
  • Live event data is worthless for the first ten to fifteen minutes. The in-play model does not beat its own frozen pre-match prediction until minute 15. That is a substantive statement about football, not about our code, and it falls straight out of the frozen-reference design.
  • Shrinkage strength tracks data scarcity exactly as the paper predicts. λ = 100 on 896 pre-match rows, λ = 0 on 17,024 in-play rows — chosen by cross-validation, at opposite ends of the grid, with no thumb on the scale.
  • Aggregate calibration metrics lie on this data. Over-confident for 75 minutes, under-confident for the last 15. One ECE number reports neither.

Three things we are not claiming: that any model beat the market; that RPS 0.024 at minute 90 is skill; that the model "converges to certainty". We measured a falling curve with a crossover at 10–15′, and that is all we will say about it.

Design consequences · the two questions the brief names

Asked and answered before you ask

What changes if snapshots are taken every minute instead of every five?
Rows go 19 → 91 per match, 28,823 → ~138,000 — but information barely moves. State variables only change on discrete events (~2.7 goals, ~4 cards per match), so adjacent snapshots differ only in the rolling recent-rate features and their autocorrelation approaches 1. Nominal n rises 4.8× while effective sample size does not. Ungrouped CV becomes more optimistic, so GroupKFold matters more, not less; naive standard errors are optimistic by roughly √(1+(m−1)ρ); compute rises ~5× for trees and ~23× for anything with a Gram matrix. The leakage machinery is unchanged — prefix_length_at is defined for any t. What we defend: the 5-minute grid wins on cost per unit information, and the right way to buy resolution is not a finer uniform grid but event-triggered snapshots — at each goal, red card and substitution, where the state actually changes.
What if SMOTE were applied to the snapshot table?
It breaks in three ways, which is why the code refuses rather than warns. (1) The synthetic rows are not game states — interpolating (min 40, 1–0, 11v11) with (min 75, 0–2, 10v11) yields minute 57.5, goal difference −0.4, 10.6 players; goal difference and man-advantage are integers and cumulative xG is monotone in minute. (2) It moves match-level label information across the match boundary: a synthetic row inherits match A's eventual result while half its feature mass comes from match B. (3) It inflates n while shrinking effective sample size, so a handful of matches dominate training and every internal CV score gets more optimistic while the model gets worse. Plus snapshot_minute is itself a feature, so interpolation produces off-grid minutes and makes the per-phase analysis incoherent. Enforced structurally: prepare_matrices raises. If forced to rebalance anyway, the defensible options are class_weight='balanced', resampling within a match or phase, or resampling whole matches carrying all 19 snapshots together.

Q&A · the rest of the ammunition

One or two sentences each

Why is your P2 a forest and not a single tree?
Both exist — HSTreeClassifier and HSForestClassifier. The single tree is the paper's interpretability argument and the cleaner object to demonstrate; the forest goes in the results table, because the comparison set is ensembles and a lone depth-limited CART would lose for reasons unrelated to HS.
Why is Task L's baseline the frozen pre-match prediction and not the prior?
Both are reported. The prior is the sanity floor; the frozen curve is the bar that justifies the in-play model existing at all. A Model 3 that merely matches the frozen curve has learned nothing from the live stream, however good its absolute RPS looks.
Kernel ridge was subsampled — isn't that cheating?
Capped at 8,000 rows, with subsampled and the actual n_train carried in the results table, so the compute row cannot be read as a full-data fit. The full cost curve is a separate deliverable: exact t ~ n^2.245, Nyström t ~ n^0.392.
Why not use the odds as a feature?
The brief permits either/or, never both in one experiment. We chose baseline, because the model-versus-market comparison is the framing of the entire project.
Did P1 actually help?
Averaged over four models it had the best RPS (0.21907) and the best calibration (ECE 0.04519), while SMOTE bought the best draw recall (0.23101) at the worst RPS (0.22283). The trade-off the brief predicts is measured, not assumed.
Your report says events are ordered by timestamp, but your code sorts by index.
The code is right and the prose is stale; it is on the fix list in TODO.md. Roughly 26 events across 24 matches carry corrupted 00:00:00 timestamps — which is precisely why the builder orders by (period, index) and repairs the clock with a running maximum.
Why these four leagues?
competition_audit.py implements the rule and produces the evidence. Single-club or partial releases (top_team_share ≥ 0.5) are excluded because opponent-side form features would be undefined for most teams; women's competitions and international tournaments are excluded because they cannot be joined to Football-Data.co.uk, and a test set that cannot be placed beside the market is meaningless in this domain.
