What matters is that the rerun finally put the analysis layer on the same data as the model layer — and once it did, several results the report was ready to state turned out to be artifacts of a small sample.

Three generations were on disk

First, the null result

Held

Adding a whole season changed nothing

Before this run, the store gained the 2025/26 season — 2,903 new matches. The split constants stayed pinned at train ≤ 2020/21, validation 21/22–22/23, test 23/24–24/25, with 2025/26 assigned to a new out_of_scope bucket that appears nowhere downstream.

Comparing all 36 model rows from Gen 2 against Gen 3: maximum absolute change in RPS is 0.0. Maximum absolute change in MAE is 0.0. Not small — zero. Train and test counts are unchanged on every row.

Why this is worth a line in the report

A season of new data entered the pipeline and moved no reported number. That is the pinned-split contract doing exactly its job, and it is a stronger reproducibility claim than any statement about random seeds.

Then, the reversals

Four conclusions that flipped when the sample grew

Reversed

Resampling: the ranking inverted end to end

On 896 training matches, synthetic minority oversampling looked like the answer and your G-SMOTENC reimplementation looked like the worst option on the board. On 39,707, the entire ordering turns over.

Best-to-worst became worst-to-best. Every synthetic oversampling arm now sits below doing nothing at all, while plain class reweighting and G-SMOTENC edge it by margins too small to defend (0.00006 and 0.00004).

Draw recall tells the same story from the other side: the best any arm achieved fell from 0.2405 to 0.0242. At 896 rows the classifier was data-starved, so fabricated minority examples genuinely helped it find draws. At 39,707 they only add noise to a boundary the model can already see.

The claim to make

Class balancing does not solve the draw problem — and the apparent case for it in the small-sample study was a symptom of the small sample. Synthetic oversampling actively costs RPS once there is enough data; reweighting is free and harmless.

Reversed

Snapshot frequency buys nothing

Sampling every 5, 10 or 15 minutes gives Task Lc RPS of 0.12596, 0.12585 and 0.12578 respectively. Coarser is marginally better. The 19-snapshot design is not purchasing resolution, and the report should not claim it does.

Correction — the ensemble verdict did not change

An earlier draft of this page said Task Lr was “the one place ensembling earns its keep” and that this settled whether to revert Lr to a plain random forest. That was wrong, and the table below is why.

The stack's significant Lr wins are over lightgbm and xgboost, which rank seventh and fifth on that head. Against random_forest — second at 0.96015 versus the stack's 0.95633 — the comparison is p = 1.000. Beating the weak members of your own ensemble is a statement about them, not a case for the ensemble.

So the verdict the earlier pack recorded still holds at 44× the data: ensembling does not beat the best single model in any of the four heads. The Lr revert question stays open, and the deciding factor is bundle size (4.5 MB against 2.4 MB), not accuracy.

Parity arrives at minute 20, half-time reads 0.15304, and the final snapshot reaches 0.02278.

This series was silently empty in earlier packs; the join between pre-match and in-play predictions returned nothing because the extended split marks those 344 matches excluded from Tasks C and R. It is populated here through the persisted serving bundle, and the coverage assertion now fails loudly rather than passing on an empty frame.

What to carry into the report and slides

  • Lead with the reversal. “Resampling helps” and “event-form features are load-bearing” were both small-sample artifacts. A study that repeats itself at 44× the data and reaches the opposite conclusion is a more interesting finding than either conclusion alone.
  • Say ensembling did not help, and now say it with power. All four ensemble-vs-best-single comparisons sit at Holm p = 1.000 on 44× the data. A null result that survives a 44× increase in sample is worth far more than one measured on 344 matches.
  • Claim the temporal stack, narrowly. stack_temporal > stack at p = 0.048 supports the design argument for fitting the meta-learner on recent validation — but say in the same breath that neither beats plain xgboost.
  • State the market honestly. Still unbeaten, gap now 0.00499 rather than 0.02477. That is the headline number for the whole project.
  • Use the score-dominance result. Dropping the live score costs 0.048 RPS; dropping in-play xG gains 0.0006. One chart carries the entire in-play feature story.
  • Do not claim snapshot frequency matters. 15-minute sampling scores marginally better than 5-minute.
  • Report serving latency. /predict p95 is 0.377 ms and p99 is 0.478 ms; /replay p95 is 1.573 ms.

Caveats to keep visible

  • Hyperparameters were searched on a subsample. Tuning used 8,000 rows for Tasks C and R, not the full 39,707. Better than Gen 1's 896, but the tuned configurations are not optimal for the final training size.
  • Tuning did not re-run in this pass. SKIP_TUNING was set, reusing best_params.json from 28 August. This is sound — Gen 2 and Gen 3 model rows are bit-identical, so the parameters match the data they are used on — but it should be stated rather than implied.
  • The margin-to-probability result is untested. It beats the direct classifier on a point estimate only; no bootstrap covers that comparison.
  • 2025/26 is loaded but unused. 2,903 matches sit in the store marked out_of_scope. Rolling them into the test window means moving all four split constants together and re-running the model layer.
  • Gen 1 and Gen 3 feature sets differ. The pre-match design matrix went from 50–75 columns to 157. Ablation and SHAP comparisons across generations are directional, not like-for-like.
